THE WHY BEHIND THE BREAD

Sourdough Science

What I've learned about the science behind sourdough and why the process works.

What is sourdough?

When I first became interested in sourdough, I thought it was mostly about following a recipe and making bread. The more I researched it, the more I realized that there is a lot more happening than I originally thought.

Sourdough is a type of bread made using a fermented mixture called a sourdough starter. A starter is made from flour and water and develops naturally occurring microorganisms over time. These microorganisms are what help make sourdough different from bread made with commercial yeast.

One thing I have learned from researching sourdough is that the process takes time. You can't always rush it, and learning to be patient is an important part of learning how to make sourdough.

What is fermentation?

Fermentation was one of the parts of sourdough that I wanted to understand better. In simple terms, fermentation is a process where microorganisms use available nutrients and produce different substances as they grow.

In sourdough, fermentation helps create some of the gases, acids, and other compounds that affect the bread. This is part of why sourdough can have its distinct flavor, smell, and texture.

What I find interesting is that a starter can look like it is just sitting there, but there is actually a lot happening that I can't see. Learning about fermentation has helped me understand why sourdough takes time instead of being something I can rush.

What's in a starter?

A sourdough starter contains naturally occurring microorganisms, including wild yeast and lactic-acid bacteria. They work together during fermentation.

The wild yeast helps produce carbon dioxide as it uses nutrients from the flour. The carbon dioxide contributes to the bubbles and gas that help the dough rise.

The lactic-acid bacteria produce acids that contribute to the flavor and environment of the starter. This is one reason sourdough can have the slightly tangy flavor that people recognize.

I think this is one of the coolest parts of sourdough because something as simple as flour and water can develop into an active starter.

Why does sourdough rise?

One of the questions I had while researching sourdough was how the bread actually rises without adding regular commercial yeast.

During fermentation, microorganisms in the starter produce carbon dioxide gas. The dough's structure can hold onto some of that gas, creating bubbles inside the dough.

As more gas is produced and trapped, the dough can expand and rise. This is why seeing bubbles and growth in a starter can be a sign that fermentation is taking place.

Learning this made the rising process make much more sense to me. Instead of just seeing the dough get bigger, I can understand what is happening behind the scenes.

Key Vocabulary

Starter A fermented mixture of flour and water that contains microorganisms and can be used to help leaven sourdough.
Hydration The amount of water compared with the amount of flour, usually expressed as a percentage.
Bulk fermentation The main fermentation period after the ingredients are mixed and before the dough is shaped.
Proofing A stage when shaped dough continues to ferment and develop before baking.
Levain A portion of an active starter that is prepared specifically to use in a bread dough.
Discard A portion of starter that is removed during feeding. It can sometimes be used in other recipes instead of being thrown away.
